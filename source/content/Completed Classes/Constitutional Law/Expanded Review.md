## Objective 1: Differences Between Civil and Criminal Law

### a. The persons involved

**Criminal Law:**
- **State vs. Person** (Government prosecutes individual)
- Prosecutor represents the state/people
- Defendant is the accused person
- State has burden of proof

**Civil Law:**
- **Person vs. Person** (Private party disputes)
- Plaintiff brings action against defendant
- Both parties are private individuals/entities
- Plaintiff has burden of proof

### b. The actions that get one involved

**Criminal Law:**
- **Crimes** - Violations of statutes that harm society
- Examples: Murder, theft, assault, DWI, drug possession
- Actions that threaten public safety and order
- Defined by criminal codes and statutes

**Civil Law:**
- **Torts** - Private wrongs between individuals
- Examples: Negligence, breach of contract, property disputes
- Actions that cause harm to private parties
- Defined by civil codes and common law

### c. The level of information required to find responsible

**Criminal Law:**
- **Beyond a Reasonable Doubt** (highest standard)
- Approximately 95-99% certainty
- Must eliminate reasonable doubt about guilt
- Protects against wrongful conviction

**Civil Law:**
- **Preponderance of Evidence** (51% standard)
- "More likely than not"
- Lower burden reflects different consequences
- Easier to prove than criminal cases

### d. The potential penalties

**Criminal Law:**
- **Fines** (monetary punishment to state)
- **Prison/Jail** (loss of liberty)
- **Death penalty** (in capital cases)
- **Probation/Parole** (supervised release)
- **Community service**
- Creates criminal record

**Civil Law:**
- **Monetary damages** (compensation to plaintiff)
- **Injunctive relief** (court orders to do/stop doing something)
- **Specific performance** (fulfill contractual obligations)
- **Restitution** (return of property/money)
- No criminal record created

## Objective 2: The Constitution of the United States

### a. What it is

**Definition:**
- The supreme law of the United States
- Framework document establishing the federal government
- Written in 1787, ratified in 1788, effective 1789
- Creates three branches of government with checks and balances
- Defines relationship between federal and state governments
- Establishes fundamental rights and freedoms

**Key Features:**
- Written constitution (unlike British system)
- Federal system dividing power between national and state governments
- Separation of powers among three branches
- System of checks and balances
- Amendment process for changes

### b. What it replaces in a monarchical system

**Replaces:**
- **Divine right of kings** - Authority comes from "We the People," not God/monarch
- **Absolute monarchy** - Limited government with defined powers
- **Royal decrees** - Laws must be passed through legislative process
- **Royal courts** - Independent judiciary separate from executive
- **Hereditary rule** - Elected officials with term limits
- **Arbitrary power** - Government bound by written law and individual rights

**Significance:**
- Popular sovereignty replaces royal sovereignty
- Rule of law replaces rule of person
- Constitutional limits replace unlimited power
- Democratic processes replace royal edicts

### c. Who is subject to its requirements

**All persons within U.S. jurisdiction:**
- **Government officials** at all levels (federal, state, local)
- **Police officers** and law enforcement
- **Judges** and court personnel
- **Legislators** and elected officials
- **Military personnel**
- **Private citizens** (in certain contexts)
- **Corporations** and other legal entities
- **Non-citizens** within U.S. borders (most protections apply)

**Key Principle:** No one is above the law, including government officials

### d. The role of the police officer in upholding it

**Primary Duties:**
- **Protect constitutional rights** of all persons
- **Follow constitutional procedures** in investigations and arrests
- **Respect due process** requirements
- **Uphold equal protection** under the law
- **Serve as guardians** of constitutional principles

**Specific Responsibilities:**
- Conduct searches and seizures within 4th Amendment limits
- Respect 5th Amendment rights during interrogations
- Ensure 6th Amendment right to counsel
- Provide equal protection regardless of race, religion, etc.
- Use force only when constitutionally justified

**Officer's Oath:** To support and defend the Constitution

### e. Component parts of the U.S. Constitution

**Main Components:**
1. **Preamble** - Statement of purpose ("We the People...")
2. **Articles I-VII** - Structure of government
   - Article I: Legislative Branch (Congress)
   - Article II: Executive Branch (President)
   - Article III: Judicial Branch (Courts)
   - Article IV: Relations between states
   - Article V: Amendment process
   - Article VI: Supremacy clause
   - Article VII: Ratification process
3. **Amendments** - Changes and additions (27 total)
   - First 10: Bill of Rights (1791)
   - Subsequent 17: Various reforms and clarifications

## Objective 3: The Bill of Rights

### a. Which Amendments are included

**The First Ten Amendments (1791):**
1. **First Amendment** - Religion, speech, press, assembly, petition
2. **Second Amendment** - Right to bear arms
3. **Third Amendment** - No quartering of soldiers
4. **Fourth Amendment** - Search and seizure protections
5. **Fifth Amendment** - Due process, self-incrimination, double jeopardy
6. **Sixth Amendment** - Right to counsel, speedy trial, jury trial
7. **Seventh Amendment** - Civil jury trials
8. **Eighth Amendment** - Cruel and unusual punishment
9. **Ninth Amendment** - Rights retained by people
10. **Tenth Amendment** - Powers reserved to states

### b. What the Bill of Rights "does," in general terms

**Primary Purpose:**
- **Protects individual rights** from government overreach
- **Limits government power** over citizens
- **Ensures fundamental freedoms** are preserved
- **Provides legal remedies** when rights are violated

**Key Functions:**
- Creates barriers against tyranny
- Establishes minimum standards for government conduct
- Protects minority rights from majority rule
- Provides foundation for civil liberties
- Guides court decisions on constitutional issues

**Originally applied only to federal government, now applies to states through 14th Amendment**

### c. Basic provisions of key Amendments applying to peace officers

#### First Amendment
**Protections:**
- **Religion** - Free exercise, no establishment
- **Speech** - Freedom of expression (with limits)
- **Press** - Freedom of media
- **Assembly** - Right to gather peacefully
- **Petition** - Right to seek government redress

**Police Applications:**
- Cannot arrest for speech alone (unless true threats, incitement)
- Must allow peaceful protests and demonstrations
- Cannot restrict recording of police in public (generally)
- Must respect religious practices during investigations
- Cannot establish religion or favor one faith over another

#### Fourth Amendment
**Text:** "The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated..."

**Key Protections:**
- **Reasonable expectation of privacy**
- **Probable cause** requirement for warrants
- **Particularity** requirement (specific description)
- **Exclusionary rule** for violations

**Police Applications:**
- Must have reasonable suspicion for stops
- Must have probable cause for arrests and searches
- Generally need warrants for searches (with exceptions)
- Evidence obtained illegally is inadmissible
- Must respect curtilage and private property rights

**Major Exceptions:**
- Search incident to arrest
- Automobile exception
- Consent searches
- Exigent circumstances
- Plain view doctrine
- Terry stops and frisks

#### Fifth Amendment
**Key Protections:**
- **Due process** - Fair legal procedures
- **Self-incrimination** - Right to remain silent
- **Double jeopardy** - Cannot be tried twice for same offense
- **Grand jury** requirement for serious crimes
- **Just compensation** for government taking of property

**Police Applications:**
- **Miranda rights** stem from self-incrimination clause
- Must give Miranda warnings for custodial interrogation
- Cannot coerce confessions
- Must respect invocation of right to remain silent
- Cannot use physical or psychological torture
- Must follow due process in all procedures

**Miranda Requirements:**
- Right to remain silent
- Anything said can be used in court
- Right to attorney
- Attorney will be provided if cannot afford one

#### Sixth Amendment
**Key Rights:**
- **Speedy trial** - Reasonable time limits
- **Public trial** - Open court proceedings
- **Impartial jury** - Fair jury selection
- **Notice of charges** - Informed of accusations
- **Confront witnesses** - Cross-examination rights
- **Compulsory process** - Subpoena witnesses
- **Right to counsel** - Attorney representation

**Police Applications:**
- Must respect right to counsel during interrogation
- Cannot interfere with attorney-client communications
- Must cease questioning when attorney requested
- Cannot delay unreasonably in bringing charges
- Must preserve evidence for defense examination
- Cannot coach witnesses or hide exculpatory evidence

**Critical Timing:**
- Right to counsel attaches at first appearance before magistrate
- Applies during custodial interrogation if invoked
- Applies during lineups after formal charges filed
- Does not apply to booking, fingerprinting, or blood draws

## Additional Constitutional Principles for Law Enforcement

### Fourteenth Amendment - Equal Protection
**Requirements:**
- **Equal protection under law** for all persons
- **Due process** applies to state/local government
- **Incorporation** of Bill of Rights to states

**Police Applications:**
- Cannot discriminate based on race, religion, gender, etc.
- Must provide equal law enforcement services
- Cannot use different standards based on protected classes
- Profiling based solely on race/ethnicity is unconstitutional

### Exclusionary Rule Applications
**When Evidence is Suppressed:**
- Obtained through illegal search or seizure
- Obtained through coerced confession
- Fruit of the poisonous tree doctrine
- Violation of Miranda rights (testimonial evidence)

**Exceptions:**
- Independent source doctrine
- Inevitable discovery rule
- Attenuation doctrine
- Good faith exception
- Impeachment use

### Missouri-Specific Constitutional Requirements

#### Missouri Constitution Article I
**Section 15** - Search and seizure protections (similar to 4th Amendment)
**Section 18(a)** - Rights of accused persons
**Section 19** - Due process and equal protection

#### Missouri Statutory Requirements
- **24-hour rule** for warrant applications
- **POST certification** required for arrest authority
- **Specific procedures** for search warrants (10-day limit)
- **RPAW** (Released Pending Application for Warrant) procedures

## Practical Application for Officers

### Constitutional Decision-Making Process
1. **Identify the constitutional issue** (4th, 5th, 6th Amendment)
2. **Determine required legal standard** (reasonable suspicion, probable cause)
3. **Consider available exceptions** to warrant requirements
4. **Follow proper procedures** to preserve admissibility
5. **Document actions** thoroughly for court review

### Common Constitutional Violations to Avoid
- **Searches without justification** or proper exceptions
- **Arrests without probable cause**
- **Interrogation without Miranda** (when required)
- **Denial of right to counsel** when properly invoked
- **Discriminatory enforcement** based on protected classes
- **Excessive force** beyond constitutional limits

### Best Practices
- **When in doubt, get a warrant**
- **Document reasonable suspicion/probable cause** clearly
- **Respect invocation of rights** immediately
- **Treat all persons equally** under the law
- **Stay current on legal developments** and training
- **Consult with prosecutors** on complex legal issues
